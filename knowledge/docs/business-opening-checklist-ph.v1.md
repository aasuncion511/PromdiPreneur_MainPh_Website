A NOTE FROM ALLAN

Build the system
before you open.
“Okay na ’yan. Bukas na tayo.” It is easy to mistake an open door for a business
that is ready to operate. The hard part starts when rent, stock, payroll, permits
and customer expectations arrive at the same time.
In 2012, I opened a Japan Surplus Appliance store in Puerto Princesa, Palawan.
There was no similar store in the city. I had a direct line to an importer in
Manila, a cost advantage and a business model inspired by hardware stores that
had lasted for decades.
The store still closed. Sales were slow, while rent, utilities and the cost of stock
sitting on shelves kept running. A promising idea did not give me the runway or
operating system I needed to sustain it.
That experience changed how I think about starting a business. Opening takes
money, permits and energy. Building a business also takes cash discipline, clear
processes and work your team can repeat without calling you for every decision.
Diskarte is a real strength: the ability to improvise, adapt and find a way
forward. Give it a structure. Document what works. Know your numbers. Set up
compliance before an inspection. Build checklists your people can use when you
are not there.
This guide is for Filipino owners opening or strengthening a micro-to-small,
brick-and-mortar business: a café, salon, water refilling station, small shop,
minimart or similar operation. Work through it before major commitments. If
you have already opened, use it to find and close the gaps.
The goal is a business with enough structure to carry the everyday work. Start
with the next unfinished step, complete it properly, and keep going.
THE PRINCIPLE

Opening a business is the start. A repeatable system helps you sustain it.

A QUICK NUMBERS REFERENCE

Three numbers
worth knowing
Use these alongside Chapter 1. Write down the assumptions, then test them
against real demand and cash timing.
01 OPENING CAPITAL

One-time opening costs + operating runway + emergency buffer

List permits, fit-out, equipment and initial stock. Then model the slow-start
period. The planning approach in this book uses three zero-revenue operating
months and a 20%–30% buffer on opening costs plus runway. Adjust the
assumptions to the actual risks.
02 UNIT CONTRIBUTION

Selling price per unit − variable cost per unit

Use the amount left after the costs that move with each sale. This contribution
pays fixed operating costs. If prices, products or margins differ, use a realistic
weighted sales mix with your accountant.
03 OPERATING BREAK-EVEN

Monthly fixed operating costs ÷ contribution per unit
Then divide monthly break-even units by operating days.

Use your actual operating days. Compare the resulting daily sales target with
observed demand. Track loan-principal payments, owner drawings, stock
purchases and other cash timing separately in the cash forecast; accounting
profit and available cash are different checks.

Keep both views
The profit view asks whether the business earns enough. The cash view asks
whether money is available when each payment falls due. Review both before
taking on a fixed commitment.

CHAPTER 01

Before You Register

The foundation and idea checklist

Before you file a form, sign a lease, or pay for fit-out, check whether the
business can work. Registration makes your decisions official. It does not prove
that customers will buy, that the location is right, or that your capital will last.
These fifteen checks help you test the idea, understand the numbers, and
prepare to run the business. Be honest about what you know and what still
needs evidence. A problem found on paper is usually easier to fix than one found
after opening.

Start here
• Start with demand, startup capital, and your daily break-even target.
• Check the location, business structure, and name before making expensive
commitments.
• Finish with a one-page business concept that brings your decisions together.

BEFORE YOU MOVE ON

• Demand is supported by observations of actual buying behavior.
• Capital covers pre-opening costs, runway, and a buffer.
• Daily break-even, 12-month scenarios, and 13-week cash flow are written
down.
• The structure, name, location, suppliers, and operating coverage have been
checked.
• Your one-page concept and Month 3, 6, and 12 targets are ready.

CHAPTER 01 • CHECKLIST 1.01

Test demand with real buying
behavior
DO THIS

Spend at least two weeks checking demand around your intended location
before making a major commitment. Look for people who could become paying
customers, then compare what you observe with the sales your business will
need.
WHY IT MATTERS

Encouragement from friends, family, and barkada can help you get started. It
does not tell you how often strangers will buy or how much they will spend. A
milk tea shop can attract plenty of praise and still struggle if nearby foot traffic
does not turn into enough daily purchases.
You need evidence that connects the idea to an actual place, price, and buying
routine. Keep friendly feedback separate from your demand estimate so it does
not make the numbers look stronger than they are.
MAKE IT PRACTICAL

Do a “shadow count”: stand near the proposed location and manually count foot
traffic at different times. Include early morning, lunch, merienda, and early
evening. Record the time and the count so you can compare visits.
Visit the nearest similar business. Observe transaction time, queue length, and
customer behavior. Look at how many passersby stop to buy, rather than
assuming every passerby is a prospect.
If there is no similar business nearby, find out why. The gap may be an
opportunity, but it may also point to weak demand or a location problem. Use
the evidence in your break-even check in Item 3.
DONE WHEN

You have two weeks of observations, a clear picture of likely buyers, and a
demand estimate supported by buying behavior rather than encouragement
alone.

CHAPTER 01 • CHECKLIST 1.02

Build your capital estimate, including
a buffer
DO THIS

Calculate the money you need to open and stay open before spending. Use three
columns: pre-opening costs, operating runway, and an emergency buffer. Fund
all three, or change the plan before committing.
WHY IT MATTERS

It is easy to count equipment, opening inventory, fit-out, and the first month’s
rent. Less visible costs can use up the money just as quickly: rent during fit-out,
permits and compliance, employer contributions, and the cost of correcting early
mistakes.
Sales may take time to build. If all your capital goes into opening day, even a
promising business can run out of cash before it develops a regular customer
base. Budgeting for slow sales gives you room to learn and adjust.
MAKE IT PRACTICAL

Column 1: Pre-opening costs. List every one-time cost before opening, including
permits, fit-out, equipment, and initial inventory. Check whether your landlord
gives you a rent-free fit-out period; do not assume one.
Column 2: Operating runway. Set aside three full months of projected operating
expenses, calculated at zero revenue. Include rent, payroll, utilities, supplier
commitments, and applicable SSS, PhilHealth, and Pag-IBIG costs.
Column 3: Emergency buffer. Add 20%–30% of Columns 1 and 2 combined. A
30% allowance is the more cautious end of this planning range.
If available capital cannot cover the three columns, delay, save more, reduce
fixed costs, or restructure the business model.
DONE WHEN

Your estimate includes opening costs, three zero-revenue months, and a
20%–30% buffer, with a realistic funding source for the total.

Check current government fees and employer-contribution requirements before
finalizing the budget.

CHAPTER 01 • CHECKLIST 1.03

Calculate your daily break-even target
DO THIS

Work out how many units you must sell each day to cover your costs before you
sign a lease or accept another fixed commitment. Then compare that target with
the demand evidence from Item 1.
WHY IT MATTERS

Break-even turns a general hope into an operating question: can this business
sell enough, at this price, on an ordinary day? You need that answer to judge the
location, pricing, and product mix.
A busy opening day can hide a weak model. The useful test is whether an
average Tuesday can support the business. Finding that the target is unrealistic
now gives you time to adjust rent, staffing, pricing, or the offer before those
decisions become expensive to reverse.
MAKE IT PRACTICAL

Add your fixed monthly operating costs, including rent, the fixed portion of
utilities, salaries, and mandatory employer contributions. Keep the assumptions
visible so you can update them later.
For each item, subtract its variable cost from its selling price. The amount left is
its contribution toward fixed costs. If you sell several products, use an average
based on the mix you expect to sell.
Divide monthly fixed costs by average contribution per item to estimate monthly
break-even units. Divide by 26 operating days if that is your schedule; otherwise
use your actual planned days.
Also allow for loan payments in your cash planning. Ask your accountant to
separate cash obligations from accounting expenses so you do not confuse
operating break-even with cash available.
DONE WHEN

You can state your monthly and daily sales targets, explain the assumptions, and
show why the location could realistically deliver them.

VERIFY BEFORE USE • Official sources

Have your accountant check contribution margins, fixed costs, and the separate
cash impact of loan payments.

CHAPTER 01 • CHECKLIST 1.04

Choose the business structure
deliberately
DO THIS

Decide on your business structure before starting registration. Compare sole
proprietorship, One Person Corporation (OPC), and a regular corporation
against your ownership plans, liability exposure, financing, and ability to
manage ongoing compliance.
WHY IT MATTERS

A sole proprietorship is commonly used by single-owner businesses, but the
business does not provide the same legal separation from its owner as a
corporation. Business debts and claims can expose personal assets.
A corporation or OPC can provide a separate legal entity, with additional
records, filings, and costs. That protection has limits; it should not be treated as
a guarantee that an owner can never be personally liable.
Changing structure later may involve new registration work and changes to
contracts, accounts, and records. Make the decision while you can compare
options without the pressure of an opening date.
MAKE IT PRACTICAL

Start with three questions: Who will own the business? What could go wrong
financially or operationally? What annual compliance work can you realistically
maintain?
For example, a single-owner, brick-and-mortar business with startup capital
below ₱1 million may consider sole proprietorship. The amount alone does not
settle suitability; compare its risks and obligations with the other structures
before deciding.
If you expect to borrow, bring in investors, or operate in a field with significant
liability exposure, such as food service, personal care, or construction, speak
with a corporate lawyer before registering.
Ask specifically about the OPC option introduced under the Revised Corporation
Code of 2019. Confirm eligibility, capital rules, exceptions, and ongoing
obligations.
DONE WHEN

You have chosen a structure, understand its personal-risk and compliance
implications, and have resolved any legal questions before filing.
VERIFY BEFORE USE • Official sources

Confirm current OPC eligibility, capital exceptions, and the legal and annual
compliance implications of each structure.

CHAPTER 01 • CHECKLIST 1.05

Audit competitors within two
kilometers
DO THIS

Visit similar businesses within a 2-kilometer radius of your intended location.
Find one customer need you can meet measurably better, then turn that
advantage into a clear operating standard.
WHY IT MATTERS

A busy competitor may show that demand exists. It does not prove that the area
can support another business offering the same thing. New entrants can end up
competing for the same customers without enough sales for everyone.
Competition itself is not a reason to stop. Entering without understanding it
makes your decisions harder. You need to know what nearby businesses charge,
where they perform well, and what customers still struggle to get.
Your business does not have to be radically different. A practical improvement
that matters to a specific customer can be a useful starting position.
MAKE IT PRACTICAL

Visit as a regular customer and order something. Observe price points, service
speed, cleanliness, staff behavior, peak hours, and the kinds of customers who
buy. Take notes soon after each visit while the details are fresh.
Compare businesses using the same questions. Where is service consistently
slow? What useful product is missing? Which part of the experience falls short of
what your intended customer needs?
Choose one gap your team can reliably address. Write it in plain language and
make it specific enough to check in daily operations.
Use that promise in your opening message, then build the staffing, stock, and
service routine needed to deliver it. Avoid promising an advantage your
operating model cannot sustain.
DONE WHEN

You have a competitor comparison, one clear customer-relevant advantage, and
a practical way to deliver and measure it.

CHAPTER 01 • CHECKLIST 1.06

Define the customer you will serve
first
DO THIS

Write one sentence describing your primary customer. Include their age range,
spending behavior or income bracket, daily routine, and the problem your
business solves. Use it to guide opening decisions.
WHY IT MATTERS

“Everyone” gives you little help when choosing products, prices, hours, or a
location. “People who want affordable food” is still too broad to tell you which
food, what price, or when those people need it.
A wide product range can feel safer because it appears to appeal to more people.
It can also scatter your attention and make it harder to become consistently
good at the offer that brings customers back.
Choose a primary customer for the opening plan. You can learn from other
buyers later without making every possible customer a priority from the start.
MAKE IT PRACTICAL

Use this example as a model: “Office workers and BPO employees aged 22–35
who want a fast, affordable lunch within walking distance of their building, with
an option beyond a carinderia or mall food court.”
That sentence gives you a basis for decisions about menu size, preparation time,
price points, opening hours, packaging, and walking distance. Rewrite it until it
does the same for your business.
Now review your proposed products, location, hours, and opening message. Ask
how each choice helps this person. If a choice does not serve that customer,
decide whether it belongs in the opening plan or can wait.
Use actual observations from your demand checks to refine the description
rather than relying only on the customer you hope to attract.
DONE WHEN

Your customer sentence is specific enough to explain your main choices about
products, prices, location, hours, and marketing.

CHAPTER 01 • CHECKLIST 1.07

Verify zoning before committing to
the space
DO THIS

Ask the relevant LGU office whether the exact address can legally support your
intended business activity. Get written confirmation before signing a lease or
committing to fit-out.
WHY IT MATTERS

An attractive space can still be the wrong place to operate. Local zoning may
classify it as residential, commercial, industrial, or mixed-use, with conditions
that affect which activities are allowed.
Previous tenants and nearby businesses are useful background, but they do not
establish that your proposed use is permitted. A landlord’s assurance that
another tenant had a permit cannot replace confirmation from the LGU.
If a zoning problem appears after you have paid for improvements, resolving it
may cost time and money or leave you with a space that cannot support your
business. Make this a location check, not a final paperwork check.
MAKE IT PRACTICAL

Visit or contact your LGU’s Zoning Administrator, Zoning Office, or Office of the
Building Official and ask which office handles the confirmation you need. Give
the exact address and describe the planned activity clearly.
Request the applicable zoning certification or written determination. Ask about
the current documents, fee, and processing time rather than assuming the visit
will be free or finished in a day.
If the property is residentially zoned but has housed commercial tenants, ask
whether a valid variance, exemption, or reclassification exists and whether it
covers your proposed activity.
Keep the written response in your location file. Resolve any conditions before
relying on the space in your launch budget or timetable.
DONE WHEN

You have written LGU confirmation for the exact address and activity, and any
conditions or required approvals are clear before you commit.

VERIFY BEFORE USE • Official sources

Confirm the zoning office, documents, fee, processing time, and conditions for
the exact address and business activity.

CHAPTER 01 • CHECKLIST 1.08

Check the name before building the
brand
DO THIS

Check your proposed name through DTI, SEC, and IPOPHL before ordering
signs, packaging, or other branded materials. Resolve potential conflicts before
you invest in recognition.
WHY IT MATTERS

A name can feel personal and memorable while already being used or protected
by another business. Discovering a conflict after you have printed materials and
built a social following creates avoidable replacement work and expense.
Business-name registration and trademark protection serve different purposes.
A name accepted by one registration system should not be treated as clearance
across every other system.
If the business may grow into a larger brand or a franchise, consider trademark
protection early. The cost of changing a name grows as more customers,
suppliers, and materials come to depend on it.
MAKE IT PRACTICAL

Check DTI’s current business-name search for sole proprietorship records. Use
the official DTI website to reach the live service rather than relying on an old
portal address.
Check the SEC’s current company-name verification service for corporations
and OPCs. Look beyond identical spelling when assessing potentially confusing
names.
Search the Intellectual Property Office of the Philippines trademark records for
the name and relevant similar marks. Ask for professional advice if the results
are unclear or the brand will be central to expansion.
The Philippines generally uses a first-to-file trademark system, but trademark
rights and objections have legal qualifications. Discuss filing early rather than
assuming that using a name first guarantees protection or that every later filing
defeats earlier rights.
DONE WHEN

You have checked all three systems, recorded any concerns, and decided
whether to seek trademark advice or file before investing further in the name.
VERIFY BEFORE USE • Official sources

Use the current official DTI, SEC, and IPOPHL search services; seek trademark
advice if similar names or rights are unclear.

CHAPTER 01 • CHECKLIST 1.09

Project the first twelve months
DO THIS

Build a 12-month financial projection in three scenarios before accepting fixed
costs. Write down your assumptions about customers, average spend, product
costs, and monthly expenses, then see when each scenario becomes viable.
WHY IT MATTERS

A projection helps you test the decisions behind the business. It does not
promise that the year will unfold as planned. Even rough numbers are useful
when you can see the assumptions and challenge them.
Without a model, you may know that the bank balance is falling without knowing
which decision needs attention. With one, you can compare actual results with
customer volume, pricing, costs, and timing to see where the gap started.
The first version can be simple. Its value comes from making the reasoning
visible and updating it as better evidence arrives.
MAKE IT PRACTICAL

Build a conservative case with traffic and sales 30% below your shadow-count
estimate. Build a base case that matches the estimate, and an optimistic case
20% above it.
Use a realistic conversion from foot traffic to paying customers in every case.
Run all three through the same cost structure and identify when cash flow
becomes positive.
Treat an optimistic case that still does not break even within nine months as a
strong prompt to review the model. Use nine months as a planning warning
point and adjust it to the economics of your business.
Review heavy fixed costs, low prices, weak margins, or unrealistic volume
assumptions. Make changes on paper and rerun the scenarios before using your
capital to test them.
DONE WHEN

You have three monthly scenarios, visible assumptions, and a clear explanation
of what must happen for the business to become viable.

CHAPTER 01 • CHECKLIST 1.10

Separate business and personal
money
DO THIS

Set up a dedicated account permitted for your business use before the first
transaction. Route business money through it, record every movement, and
establish a regular owner’s draw appropriate to your structure.
WHY IT MATTERS

When household and business money share the same account or wallet, it
becomes difficult to tell whether the business pays its own way. Personal savings
can quietly cover operating gaps while daily sales make things feel “okay
naman.”
Clear separation lets you see what the business earns, spends, borrows, and
receives from you. It also gives your accountant a cleaner record and helps you
make decisions using the actual business position.
This discipline matters while the business is small. Waiting until it stabilizes can
leave months of mixed transactions to reconstruct later.
MAKE IT PRACTICAL

Ask a suitable bank or financial service which account supports your business
structure and intended transactions. A rural bank or a digital provider may be
practical, but verify the account’s terms and requirements rather than assuming
a personal savings account is suitable.
If considering providers such as Maya Business or UnionDigital, check their
current products, eligibility rules, and permitted uses directly before choosing
an account.
Choose a fixed amount and regular schedule for owner withdrawals instead of
taking cash whenever a personal expense appears. Have your accountant
explain the correct treatment for your business structure.
Record money you put into the business separately from customer sales. Record
business expenses and owner withdrawals clearly too. The point is to make
every peso entering or leaving the business traceable.
DONE WHEN

Business transactions have a separate, suitable account and clear records, with
a regular withdrawal routine and no unexplained mixing of personal spending.
VERIFY BEFORE USE • Official sources

Confirm the account permits your business use and that the withdrawal method
suits your business structure.

CHAPTER 01 • CHECKLIST 1.11

Get supplier terms in writing
DO THIS

Confirm key supplier terms before using them in your pricing or inventory plan.
Keep the written record and identify a backup for every input your business
cannot go without for more than 48 hours.
WHY IT MATTERS

A friendly conversation can leave important details unresolved. Minimum
orders, lead times, payment terms, return policies, and price changes all affect
how much stock and cash you need.
For example, a water refilling station may plan around a three-day chemical
delivery. If the real lead time becomes seven to ten days during peak season, the
business can run short just as customers begin relying on it.
A supplier promise is more useful when both sides can refer to the same terms.
You do not need to wait for a problem before asking for clarity.
MAKE IT PRACTICAL

Get written confirmation by formal letter, email, or a documented text exchange.
For each key supplier, cover four essentials: unit price and minimum order
quantity; payment terms, including COD, net-15, or net-30; delivery lead time in
normal and peak periods; and the return or replacement policy for defective or
damaged deliveries.
Also ask how and when prices can change, because your selling price may
depend on those inputs. Update your costing when confirmed terms differ from
the original assumption.
Identify at least one alternative supplier for each critical input you cannot
operate without for more than 48 hours. Check that the alternative can actually
supply what you need.
Keep these details where the person ordering stock can find them.
DONE WHEN

Your pricing and stock plan use confirmed supplier terms, and each critical input
has an identified backup source.

CHAPTER 01 • CHECKLIST 1.12

Plan for daily operations and your
absence
DO THIS

Check whether you have the time, people, and routines to operate the business
after opening day. Write down how it will work during the first 90 days and
during a two-week absence.
WHY IT MATTERS

Opening is a project with a budget, deadline, and visible finish line. Operating
requires daily repetition: checking stock, serving customers, managing cash,
supporting staff, and handling problems.
Being good at preparing the opening does not automatically mean those routines
are ready. If you cannot be present and no trained person or clear process can
take over, small decisions can quickly stall the business.
You are looking for an operating plan that ordinary employees can follow. It
should not depend on a family member knowing what you meant or on you
answering every phone call.
MAKE IT PRACTICAL

Answer three questions in writing. First: can you be physically present for the
first 90 days? If not, who will manage, and when will that person be found and
trained?
Second: do you have, or can you finish before opening, a daily operations
checklist that a non-family employee can follow without asking you about every
decision? Include what must happen and who is responsible.
Third: what happens if you are sick or unavailable for two weeks? Identify who
takes over routine work and which decisions need escalation.
If an answer is “I’ll work it out later,” treat that part of the operating model as
unfinished. Build the missing coverage or routine into pre-opening preparation
while you still have time to train and test it.
DONE WHEN

You have documented first-90-day coverage, a usable daily checklist, and a
two-week absence plan with a responsible person for essential work.

Forecast cash week by week
DO THIS

Build a 13-week cash-flow forecast before opening and update it every week
afterward. List expected cash receipts and payments by the week they will
actually arrive or fall due.
WHY IT MATTERS

Sales and available cash can move on different schedules. A business can record
revenue and still struggle to pay rent, wages, suppliers, and government
contributions when those payments become due.
Customer payment delays, stock purchases, and seasonal slowdowns can leave a
gap between earning sales and having usable money in the account. Several
large obligations falling in the same week can make that gap more serious.
The cash drawer at closing time does not show all the commitments already
made. A forecast helps you see those commitments before they become urgent.
MAKE IT PRACTICAL

Create one column for each of the next 13 weeks. Start with available cash, add
the cash you expect to collect, and subtract known payments. Carry the ending
balance into the following week.
Include expected customer receipts and other committed inflows. List rent,
payroll, suppliers, loan payments, taxes, and contributions according to their
actual timing. Avoid treating an unpaid sale as cash already available.
Flag weeks where outflows exceed inflows, then check whether the opening
balance can cover the gap. Plan deliberately for any shortfall rather than hoping
the next week’s sales arrive early.
Use the forecast to test the runway in Item 2. Update it each week as collection
dates, orders, and obligations change, so it remains a working tool rather than
an opening exercise.

Pay for fit-out in checked milestones
DO THIS

Agree a written milestone-payment schedule with the contractor before work
begins. Tie progress payments to defined completed work and inspection, and
retain a final amount until defects on the punch list are resolved.
WHY IT MATTERS

A contractor may request 50% or more upfront. Large advance payments put
more of your money at risk before you have seen the work. A staged schedule
gives both sides clearer expectations and keeps the remaining payments linked
to actual progress.
MAKE IT PRACTICAL

Use this illustrative structure as a starting point: 30% on contract signing and
mobilization; 40% after completion and inspection of major structural, electrical,
and tiling work; 20% at substantial completion of visible and functional work;
and the final 10% after walkthrough, punch-list completion, and sign-off.
Define what each milestone includes. Broad labels such as “half finished” can
create arguments. Specify the work you expect to see, the inspection or
checking process, and who confirms completion before the payment becomes
due.
Write the final-retention arrangement into the contract. A punch list is the list of
incomplete or defective items to resolve. Agree how it will be recorded and
checked so the final walkthrough becomes a practical acceptance process rather
than a rushed request for the balance.
Compare the payment schedule with your cash plan and rent commencement
date. Contractor payments, equipment purchases, and opening reserves must fit
together. Staged payments do not guarantee timely or good work, so keep
checking progress and workmanship throughout the project.
DONE WHEN

The signed fit-out contract states the payment amounts, measurable milestones,
inspection process, final punch-list requirement, and conditions for releasing the
last 10% or other agreed retention.

Separate business money from day
one
DO THIS

Open a dedicated business bank account before the first business transaction.
Plan the application alongside registration so the account is ready when
customers and suppliers begin moving money.
WHY IT MATTERS

When personal and business transactions share one account, it is harder to tell
whether the business is profitable or quietly using your savings. Reconstructing
those movements later creates extra bookkeeping and can make supporting your
records during a tax review more difficult.
MAKE IT PRACTICAL

Aim to begin the account-opening process in the same week you complete BIR
registration, subject to the bank’s document requirements. Do not rely on a
promise to separate the money “once things settle down.” Each extra mixed
transaction adds another item to untangle.
Use the business account details consistently for customer payments and
supplier transactions. Keep personal purchases out of it. Where the owner
contributes money to the business, record what the transfer represents so it is
not confused with sales.
Set a fixed, scheduled transfer to your personal account for owner pay or
drawings, using the treatment your accountant recommends for your business
structure. Avoid taking unrecorded cash whenever a personal expense comes
up.
Check the account regularly against your records. Separation gives you a clean
starting point; you still need to record receipts, expenses, transfers, and
balances. If a mixed transaction happens, document and correct its treatment
promptly rather than leaving it for year-end.
DONE WHEN

The account is operational before trading, customers and suppliers have the
correct payment details, and owner transfers have a schedule and clear
accounting treatment.

CHAPTER 04 • CHECKLIST 4.07

Check suppliers on four essentials
DO THIS

Evaluate every prospective supplier before the first order using four criteria:
reliability, price stability, minimum-order flexibility, and replacement policy. Get
the important answers in writing so you can compare suppliers consistently.
WHY IT MATTERS

A nearby supplier, a personal connection, or a low introductory price may be
useful, but none proves dependable service. Late deliveries, unexpected price
changes, and unresolved damaged stock can interrupt sales and disappoint
customers even when the quoted price looks attractive.
MAKE IT PRACTICAL

Reliability: ask whether the supplier can consistently deliver the agreed
products, quantities, and dates. Request two existing customer references and
ask about routine service, not only the first order. A good sales conversation is
different from a dependable delivery record.
Price stability: ask how often prices change, how much notice is given, and how
revisions are communicated. Record the price basis so you can identify changes
between the order and delivery.
Minimum-order flexibility: compare the required quantity with your storage
space, cash cycle, and expected demand. A low unit price may still be a poor
deal if the minimum order ties up money or stock you cannot use quickly.
Replacement policy: ask how damaged, defective, or short-delivered goods are
reported and resolved, and how quickly replacements or other remedies are
provided. Record the contact person and the evidence they require. Keep all four
answers with the supplier’s quotation before committing to the order.
DONE WHEN

You have written answers to all four criteria, have checked available references,
and can explain why the supplier fits your operation beyond price or personal
familiarity.

DO THIS

After 60 to 90 days of consistent, on-time cash-on-delivery payments, ask key
suppliers whether they can offer suitable payment terms. Use your actual order
and payment record to support the conversation.
WHY IT MATTERS

Under cash on delivery (COD), you pay before you have sold the stock. With a
seven-to-fourteen-day inventory turn, money stays tied up while you wait for
sales. Appropriate terms can reduce that timing pressure, but they also create a
future payment commitment.
MAKE IT PRACTICAL

Frame the request around a dependable trading relationship: “We have been
consistent with payments and would like to discuss net-15 or net-30 terms to
manage ordering volume and grow the account.” These are negotiation options,
not terms every distributor will offer.
Ask how the due date is calculated, whether there is a credit limit, and whether
prices, discounts, or fees differ from COD. Confirm the consequences of late
payment. A longer payment period is useful only if the full arrangement works
for the business.
Check the proposed due dates against your sales and collection cycle. Do not
increase orders merely because payment is delayed. Plan for the bill when it
falls due and preserve the reliable payment behavior that made the conversation
possible.
Get any agreement in a letter, email, or signed terms sheet. Keep it with the
supplier record so a replacement sales representative can see the same terms. If
credit is declined, continue with a workable COD plan rather than assuming it
will soon become available.
DONE WHEN

Any approved terms are documented, their due dates and costs are clear, and
your purchasing plan can meet the resulting obligations without relying on
hoped-for sales.

DO THIS

Identify your three most critical inputs: the items whose absence could stop or
seriously disrupt operations within 48 hours. Establish at least one workable
alternative supplier for each before you need an emergency order.
WHY IT MATTERS

A supplier can face shortages, vehicle problems, its own payment disputes, or
pressure to prioritize larger customers. If you depend on only one source, its
problem quickly becomes yours. A backup relationship gives you another option
when the primary source cannot deliver.
MAKE IT PRACTICAL

Look at the operation rather than just the biggest spend. For a milk tea shop, tea
base or fresh milk may be critical. For a minimart, the key dependency may be
its fastest-moving products. For a water-refilling operation, identify the
treatment or other inputs your actual process requires.

without each. Use the two-day threshold as a practical test, not a claim that
every business has identical risks. Check which shortages would affect your core
customer promise.
Place a small test order with each backup where practical. This establishes the
trading relationship, checks the product against your standards, and tests
delivery and payment arrangements. A saved phone number alone does not
confirm that the supplier can serve you.
Keep the backup’s current contact details, normal lead time, minimum order,
and acceptable product specifications with the purchasing records. Know how to
activate the alternative quickly when needed. Redundancy is preparation, not an
accusation that your main supplier will fail.
DONE WHEN

Your three critical inputs each have a tested or otherwise verified backup, and
the person ordering stock knows when and how to use it.

Use a purchase order for every order
DO THIS

Create a simple purchase-order template before ordering opening stock. A
spreadsheet or printed form is enough if it captures the agreed details and is
used consistently for every supplier order.
WHY IT MATTERS

Without an order record, it is difficult to tell whether the delivery matches what

MAKE IT PRACTICAL

supplier name, and expected delivery date. List each item separately with a

person receiving goods. If the order changes, make sure the receiving copy
reflects the confirmed change rather than relying on an unexplained message or
memory.

supplier’s delivery receipt. Check actual quantities, item specifications, and
prices. Record shortages, substitutions, damage, or other discrepancies on the
receipt before signing and follow the agreed resolution process.

transaction later. Set aside an initial 30 minutes to draft a basic template, then
allow extra time to teach the checking routine. The consistent habit matters
more than the appearance of the form.

CHAPTER 04 • CHECKLIST 4.12

Price from the full cost of stock
DO THIS

Calculate the per-unit cost of getting each product ready for sale before setting
its selling price. Start with every product category, then examine individual
items where their costs or margins differ materially.
WHY IT MATTERS

The supplier’s unit price is only part of the cost. Delivery, packaging, spoilage or
damage, and financing can reduce the margin you expected. A markup that
looks attractive on the quoted price may leave too little after those costs are
included.
MAKE IT PRACTICAL

Use a clear costing worksheet: supplier unit price, plus allocated delivery cost,
plus required packaging, plus a spoilage or damage allowance where relevant,
plus applicable financing cost. Express each component as a cost per saleable
unit before adding it.
For perishables, document the spoilage assumption and the quantity you
realistically expect to sell. Do not add a percentage directly to a peso amount;
convert the assumption into a per-unit peso allowance using a consistent
method. Review it against actual results once trading begins.
If stock is bought on credit or with borrowed funds, include the relevant cost in
your pricing analysis. Ask your accountant how these management-costing items
should be treated in the financial records; the pricing worksheet and formal
inventory valuation may not be identical.
Calculate your intended markup against the full cost, then compare the selling
price with what customers will reasonably pay. For a minimart or broad retail
range, this can expose low-margin categories before you tie up cash filling the
shelves. Recheck when delivery costs, supplier prices, or wastage change.

Protect working capital before launch
spending
DO THIS

Set aside the cash needed to keep the business operating before you budget
fit-out upgrades, opening decorations, or other discretionary launch costs.
WHY IT MATTERS

Working capital covers the timing gap between money going out and customer
payments coming in. Suppliers may require cash on delivery, payroll arrives on
schedule, and a small equipment failure may need immediate attention. If the
reserve is simply whatever remains after setup, you may reach opening day with
attractive premises and too little cash to run them. Protecting the reserve first
makes the limits of the rest of the budget visible.
MAKE IT PRACTICAL

expenses. Consider aiming for six months where competition is strong, the
location is untested, or the business is highly seasonal. These are planning
buffers, not universal minimums; test whether stock purchases, payment terms,
and other cash needs require more.
Keep the allocation separate and label its purpose clearly. Budget fit-out,
inventory, and optional launch elements from the capital left after the reserve is
funded. Do not treat it as available cash for a nicer POS system or temporary
borrowing that depends on next month’s hoped-for sales.
Write the conditions for using it: covering planned operating shortfalls and
necessary disruptions while the business moves toward sustainable cash
generation. Pair every planned draw with the weekly cash-flow map in Item 5.
Review the balance frequently so you can see how much runway remains and
respond before it is exhausted.

The reserve is separately funded, its permitted uses are clear, and the remaining
launch budget can be met without quietly borrowing from it.

Separate setup costs from ongoing
obligations
DO THIS

Prepare a two-part pre-launch budget: one-time setup spending and the cash
needed for the first three months of operations. Fund both before committing to
optional extras.
WHY IT MATTERS

Pang-puhunan can become one large mental bucket that hides very different
commitments. Equipment and signage may be bought once, while rent, utilities,
payroll, supplier payments, and contributions keep returning. Some costs also
contain both elements: an initial inventory purchase is a launch outlay, but
replenishment continues after opening. Separating the timing helps you see
whether the business is fully funded rather than merely ready to look open.
MAKE IT PRACTICAL

Column A: list the one-time or initial pre-opening outlays, including fit-out,
equipment, signage, initial inventory, registration expenses, and essential
opening costs. Identify anything that also has a later renewal, replacement, or
subscription cost so it is not mistaken for a permanent one-off.
Column B: list the first three months of recurring obligations. For steady
monthly costs, multiply by three; for variable purchases and unusual payment
dates, use the expected cash schedule instead. Reconcile this amount to your
working-capital reserve so you do not double-count funding or omit a real
payment.
Only the capital remaining after both columns are funded is available for
contingencies and optional launch additions. If there is no discretionary balance,
remove the extras. If essential setup or the operating runway is still unfunded,
reduce the launch scope or move the date. Do not solve a fit-out overrun by
silently cutting the reserve.

DO THIS

Plan at least three consecutive dry-run days using real products, real

WHY IT MATTERS

pressure points you need to discover. Problems often appear when several
customers arrive together, an unusual question interrupts the queue, or opening

compliant transaction process.

operating position. Check queue length, equipment, kitchen or service

debrief. Record each gap, assign an owner, agree the fix, and confirm it has

Make referrals easy and trackable
DO THIS

Choose one simple referral mechanism before opening and include it in the
normal customer interaction, with a clear offer and a way to measure use.
WHY IT MATTERS

Happy customers may recommend you, but an easy prompt can help them know
what to share and what the referred person should do. In a local business, a
recommendation from someone nearby can be valuable because it carries
personal experience. Do not assume it always outperforms paid advertising or
needs no management. The offer still needs to be affordable, understandable,
and consistently honored.
MAKE IT PRACTICAL

A practical starting point is a physical referral card with the business name,
location or contact details, a clear reward, and a unique tracking code. Define
who receives the reward and what event qualifies: for example, a referred
customer visiting and presenting the card under the stated terms. Cost the
reward before printing.

transaction. Explain the benefit briefly, without pressuring customers. Think
about your first 50 customers: nearby early buyers may introduce the business
to their networks, but referrals still depend on a good experience and an offer
worth sharing.

counted incorrectly. Review which referrals lead to real customers and whether
the margin supports the offer. Other options include a bring-a-friend offer or a
genuinely shareable community benefit, but start with one mechanism the team
can execute reliably before adding complexity.

before you open
DO THIS

Document the recurring work in plain language, test it with someone who will
use it, and correct the gaps before opening the doors.
WHY IT MATTERS

Diskarte is useful when an unexpected problem needs a practical response.
Daily operations also need repeatable instructions so every decision does not
wait for the owner. A promising idea, adequate capital, a favorable location, and
community support still need dependable execution. Written procedures help
the team carry out the plan and give you a place to capture improvements as
real trading reveals what needs to change.
MAKE IT PRACTICAL

payment, invoice issuance, and cash reconciliation at close. Record how stock is
counted, how reorder points trigger action, how purchase orders are approved,
and how deliveries are received and checked against the order.

and daily cleaning and maintenance standards. Give each task an owner, the
needed inputs, the expected result, and a route for exceptions. Link the short
shift checklists to fuller instructions where a task needs more detail.

rehearsal. Watch where they hesitate, guess, or need to call you. Clarify those
points and test again. The goal is reliable routine work and sensible escalation,
not a claim that nobody will ever have a question. Keep the first version usable,
then revise it with evidence from daily operations and the 90-day review.
